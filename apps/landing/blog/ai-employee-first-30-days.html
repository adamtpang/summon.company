Your AI employee's first 30 days: a playbook

What to assign, what to approve, and what to leave alone. Built from six months of running Summon on AI employees — including the mistakes.

Most founders who hire their first AI employee make the same two mistakes: they either assign nothing concrete ("just help where you can") or they assign everything at once and drown in approval pings. Neither works.

The first 30 days are about calibration. You're learning what the AI employee does well, the AI employee is learning your standards, and the approval gate is doing the safety work so nothing irreversible slips through. Here is the playbook we used and the one we recommend to every Summon founder.

Week 1: one task, one output

Pick the single most repeatable task in the relevant department. It should have a clear, checkable output and no external consequence if done imperfectly. Good starting points:

  • Marketing: a weekly competitive digest — three competitors, what they shipped, what the positioning implication is.
  • Sales: a list of 20 leads matching your ICP, sourced and scored, not yet contacted.
  • Operations: a written SOP for the most manual thing you do each week.
  • Support: draft replies to the last ten support tickets (you send, or don't).
  • Finance: a categorized breakdown of last month's spending against budget.

One task. One clear output. Review it yourself at the end of the week. The output quality will surprise you — and the surprises (good and bad) tell you exactly where to calibrate next.

The approval gate in week 1: Nothing goes out. Every output is a draft for your review. The AI employee knows this from the start — it's built into how Summon works. You expand permissions later; you don't start with them.

Week 2: add a second lane, set your first spend limit

If week 1 output met your bar, add one more recurring task. Simultaneously, set your first real spend limit — even a small one. Seeing the AI employee request approval for a $12 tool subscription, explain why it needs it, and wait for your go/no-go tells you more about how the system works than any demo ever will.

The right spend limit for week 2 is low: $25–$50 per approval request, with a $100/month ceiling. You're not constraining capability — you're building trust through small verified bets.

Week 3: let it run cross-department for the first time

Real companies require coordination. Your AI Marketing employee needs to know what Engineering shipped. Your AI Sales employee needs to know what Support is hearing. In week 3, give your first AI employee one cross-department task: compile a briefing from another department's work log and write the downstream implication for their own lane.

This is when you'll see the compounding effect for the first time. A Marketing employee who reads the ship log produces better content. A Sales employee who reads support tickets writes better objection-handling. The integration is the lever.

Week 4: expand scope, then measure

By week four you have three or four weeks of output to review. Now ask: what is this AI employee doing that I would have done myself? What is it doing better? What still needs my judgment?

Map the answers into three buckets:

  • Delegate fully: tasks where the AI employee's output is consistently better than what you'd produce in the same time.
  • Delegate with gate: tasks where the output is good but the stakes are high enough to keep an approval touch.
  • Keep for now: tasks that require judgment you haven't yet seen the AI employee exercise reliably.

This is your operating model. It will shift over time — more into "delegate fully" as the AI employee builds a track record, fewer in "keep for now" as you expand scope. Review it monthly, not weekly. Micromanagement is as wasteful with AI employees as it is with human ones.

The one rule that makes all of this work

Every irreversible action — a sent email, a published post, a paid invoice, a hired contractor — requires your explicit approval before it happens. This is not an option in Summon; it is the architecture. The AI employee drafts, requests, and waits. You approve or redirect. Nothing fires without your go.

This rule is why you can run a full department on a $99/month AI employee without fear. The downside of any single mistake is bounded. The upside — 24/7 output, compounding across months — is not.

What 30 days produces: In practice, a Summon founder running one AI employee for a month ends up with 40–80 deliverables reviewed and approved, a clear picture of where the AI employee adds leverage, and two or three "I can't believe this took me five minutes to approve" moments that reshape how they think about delegation.

Common mistakes to avoid

Assigning open-ended mandates

"Grow our marketing presence" is not a task. "Write one blog post per week from the ship log, draft-to-review in Slack, live only after I approve" is a task. Specificity is not micromanagement — it is the prerequisite for delegation.

Skipping the approval log

Every approved action is evidence. After 30 days, your approval log tells you exactly what your AI employee costs, what it produces, and whether the ROI justifies expanding scope. Founders who don't read the log miss the data that makes the next hire obvious.

Hiring the second before the first is calibrated

It is tempting to hire a full AI team in week one. Resist it. One AI employee fully calibrated is worth five AI employees running without clear scope. The second hire is easy after the first is running well — you know the playbook.

What comes after 30 days

Month two is where the compounding starts to feel real. The AI employee has context — it remembers prior decisions, knows your approval patterns, has a track record it is working to maintain. Output quality rises. Your review time drops. The ratio shifts from 80% you, 20% AI to something closer to 50/50 for the tasks you've delegated.

Month three is when most Summon founders hire their second AI employee. Not because they ran out of tasks for the first — because they see a second function that could compound the same way, and now they know how to onboard it in a week instead of a month.

The playbook repeats. The leverage compounds. The founder's job becomes what it was always supposed to be: the board, not the worker.
